Anthropic files to go public on Nasdaq as “ANT,” showing $16.3 billion in half-year revenue

Anthropic, the company behind Claude, publicly filed its S-1 registration statement with the SEC on Monday for an initial public offering of Class A stock, and plans to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the symbol “ANT.” The number of shares and the price range have not been set yet. The filing shows revenue of $16.29 billion for the six months ended June 30, up 1,304% from $1.16 billion a year earlier, and $4.59 billion for all of 2025. It also shows a first-half net loss of $80.7 billion, but CNBC notes about $72 billion of that is an accounting charge from the rising value of convertible notes as the company’s valuation climbed. Anthropic stays a public benefit corporation: its Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board through a special class of stock, and a co-founders’ LLC will hold a single share carrying 50.1% of the general voting power. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are handling the prospectus, and the filing lifted chip stocks on Monday.

Microsoft offers voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 workers as it pays for AI

Microsoft is offering voluntary exit packages to about 60,000 employees, more than a quarter of the roughly 223,000 people it employed at the end of June, as it tries to contain costs while funding AI. Chief people officer Amy Coleman told staff the offer covers participating teams in the U.S., Canada, India, and the U.K. at level 70 and below, with terms similar to Microsoft’s standard severance plus career-planning help. Some sales and engineering teams are excluded, and the company calls the program time-limited.

Banks and Blackstone line up $63 billion to buy Broadcom chips for Anthropic

Bank of America, Blackstone, Citi, and Morgan Stanley will finance a $63 billion purchase of Broadcom AI chips and networking gear through a new special-purpose vehicle, then lease the equipment to Anthropic, with deliveries expected to start in 2027. The three banks are arranging the first-lien piece, Blackstone is providing the committed second-lien piece, and Broadcom is backstopping the residual value on the first lien. Broadcom shares rose more than 2% in premarket trading Tuesday on the deal, TipRanks reports.

OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Terra uses your computer at half the price

OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Terra, a fast, mid-size model for visual reasoning, computer use, and everyday writing. Its API price is $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens, half that of GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-6.1 Sol. On OpenAI’s Agents’ Last Exam computer-use benchmark, it says Terra scores 15 points higher than GPT-5.6 Terra at medium effort and matches GPT-6 Astra at a tenth of the price per token, while finishing tasks over three times faster. Its error rate on OpenAI’s hallucination test fell from 53.6% to 16.7% at low effort, and it more than doubles GPT-5.6 Terra on Terminal-Bench 4.0. These are OpenAI’s own numbers. It’s live in ChatGPT Work, Codex, and the API as gpt-6.1-terra.

Moonshot’s open-weight Kimi K3.1 Pro goes toe-to-toe with the frontier on coding

Moonshot AI released Kimi K3.1 Pro on October 23, the first model in its K3.1 series: a post-trained Kimi K3 with more reinforcement-learning compute, a 1-million-token context window, and a Highspeed variant that runs two to three times faster. Moonshot reports 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, against 58.2% for GPT-6 Astra and 66.4% for Claude Opus 5.5, plus 92.7% on BenchCAD with tools. Those scores are self-reported.

Innovation

One learned brain lets a humanoid climb ladders, vault obstacles, and get back up

Researchers at ETH Zurich and collaborators built Attention-based Contact Planning, a control approach that plans where a humanoid’s hands and feet should touch the world instead of scripting every move. Running a single learned policy on a Unitree G1, the robot climbed ladders and stairs, crossed stepping stones, crawled, vaulted obstacles, and recovered from falls, with no reference motions needed. In simulation it averaged 92.5% success across eight obstacle courses, versus 58.8% for a comparison method; those are simulated, not real-world, rates.

Innovation

Hug the box: an Amazon-led team teaches humanoids to carry with their whole body

Researchers from Amazon, UC San Diego, and the University of Michigan published Whole-Body Dexterity, a method that lets a humanoid hold objects against its torso or under an arm, not just in its hands. They generated about 2.5 million simulated grasps, trained expert policies, and distilled them into one controller. In real tests on a Unitree G1, run by a human teleoperator in a motion-capture suit with the controller keeping the object supported, it completed 45 of 50 trials, versus 18% for direct motion retargeting. The work is an arXiv preprint.

“I hardly used my phone before”: a Nepal AI class turns beginners into builders

When cooking gas ran short, Bina Nagarkoti, a ward official in Godawari, bought an induction cooker she didn’t know how to use, so she photographed it and asked ChatGPT, a trick from a three-month-old AI literacy class. Now she uses AI to draft notices and proposals and studied with it for her Grade 12 exams, returning to school after 25 years. Classmates in the 19-person Lalitpur program include a ride-hailing driver making videos, a wheelchair basketball player who now writes proposals on his own, and a bank employee who cut a three-hour records check to one. Her lesson: “its answers are not always correct. You have to check them yourself.”

No laptop, no problem: 400,000 students learn AI on their phones

India’s CodeYogi Foundation runs a free, 30-day AI-fluency program over WhatsApp, with a browser code editor that works on a phone and lessons in local languages. As of August it reached more than 400,000 students across 4,200 government schools in nine states. A 17-year-old in Saharanpur is building a web app to teach Vedic math, and an arts student in Churu is designing a marketing platform for rural women entrepreneurs, both without owning a laptop. Among the earlier coding program’s top students, 59% came from non-STEM backgrounds.
