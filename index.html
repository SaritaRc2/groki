White House reverses a 17-day freeze and restores Britain’s AI Security Institute access to pre-release OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind models

As of Sunday, October 11, the White House has reinstated the UK’s AI Security Institute (AISI) access to pre-release frontier models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind, The Telegraph reports (via AOL and CryptoBriefing). On September 24 the Trump administration’s Office of the National Cyber Director had asked OpenAI and Anthropic to stop sharing new models with AISI pending a U.S. national security review; the freeze lasted 17 days. During it, Anthropic kept Claude Mythos 5.1 out of UK review and limited it to U.S. organizations. The reversal followed lobbying by UK AI minister Kanishka Narayan. AISI was set up after the 2023 AI safety summit to evaluate advanced systems before public release. Whether the national security review is finished, and whether Anthropic will broaden Mythos 5.1 beyond U.S. access, remains unclear.

Models

Microsoft-Decision-1 doesn’t write, it decides, and Microsoft clocks it at 35 times faster than GPT-6 Sol

Microsoft on October 9 introduced Microsoft-Decision-1, a fast “decision-scoring” model available in Microsoft Foundry and through OpenRouter. Instead of generating text, it takes a fixed set of answer options (yes/no, multiple choice, ratings, or rubric grades of AI responses and agent actions) and returns a calibrated probability for each, for jobs like routing, classification, prioritization, verification, and workflow control. Microsoft post-trained it from Qwen3.5-9B and says it will soon rebase it on other models, including Microsoft AI (MAI) and OpenAI. In Microsoft’s 36-benchmark comparison, spanning nearly 150,000 questions kept blind from training, it had the highest accuracy and was the fastest measured: 2.5 times quicker than runner-up H2O-Lightning-4B v1.1 and 35 times quicker than GPT-6 Sol. When the same request is perturbed in eight ways, it changes its decision 1.3% of the time on average. Input costs $0.042 per million tokens, and output tokens are free.

TypeSafe AI, maker of the non-text model Jev, raises $870 million at a $7.5 billion valuation weeks after launch

TypeSafe AI, the developer of Jev, has raised $870 million at a $7.5 billion valuation in a round led by Andreessen Horowitz, with Sequoia and existing investor DCVC, TechCrunch reports. Jev went viral almost instantly after its September 15 release, and the startup claims a third of Fortune 500 companies already use it. Jev is built on a transformer architecture but is not a large language model: it doesn’t output text, and instead produces probabilities, which the company calls “calibrated decisions.” TypeSafe claims it works significantly faster and uses far fewer tokens than LLMs, and positions it for automating tasks rather than generating text or code. “We have been super good at human language for four years, but it’s not useful for automation because computers speak a different language,” co-founder Diogo Almeida, a former OpenAI researcher, told TechCrunch last month. TypeSafe was co-founded in 2024 by Almeida, former Meta research engineer Sasha Sheng, and Erik Gafni.

Innovation

DeepMind’s AlphaProof Nexus solves dozens of open math problems and lands a peer-reviewed Science paper

Google DeepMind’s AlphaProof Nexus solved dozens of open mathematics problems on its own, according to a new study in Science reported October 12 by The Decoder. It cracked nine of 353 open Erdős problems—two unsolved for 56 years—and the team shared those solutions online ahead of the paper. It also proved 44 conjectures from the OEIS integer sequence database, solved an open question in algebraic geometry, and improved a known bound in min-max optimization. The system is built around Gemini 3.1 Pro, which drafts and revises proofs in the formal language Lean; Lean checks each draft after every edit, and for harder sub-problems it can call on AlphaProof. Even without AlphaProof it solved the nine Erdős problems, DeepMind reports. The paper arrives amid debate over AI math: OpenAI recently posted hundreds of AI proofs on GitHub instead of journals, while DeepMind put its work through peer review.

Innovation

DOE, NIH, and Biohub team up to build the open data for AI models that predict how cells behave

The Department of Energy’s Office of Science, the National Institutes of Health, and Biohub signed a memorandum of understanding on October 9 to build an open biological data foundation for predictive AI models of living systems that can accurately predict how cells respond to interventions, according to DOE’s announcement. DOE is investing more than $500 million over five years in fundamental cell research spanning data collection, AI analytics, lab measurement and imaging, modeling, and computation, and Biohub is investing $500 million over five years in its Virtual Biology Initiative. The work joins DOE national labs’ exascale supercomputers and user facilities such as the Joint Genome Institute, AI-driven robotic “autonomous laboratories,” Biohub’s AI technologies, and NIH’s health data and imaging, as part of the Genesis Mission. “An accurate predictive model of biology could dramatically accelerate scientific discovery by enabling scientists to perform experiments digitally,” said Biohub head of science Alex Rives.

Motivation

No computers, no internet, so an ASU student taught AI literacy in rural Kenya with storybooks instead

Martin Ndegwa, an Arizona State University master’s student and Mastercard Foundation Scholar, went home to Kenya to teach AI and data literacy, ASU News reports, but found that some rural schools in Kilifi County lacked reliable electricity, computers, or internet, and for some communities the nearest library services were more than 30 kilometers away. “When you go to the field, you have to listen to what the field requires,” he says. So he pivoted: with local partners he helped set up Shomani Reading Clubs in six rural primary schools, and his team used AI to help create two illustrated printed storybooks. In “Zuri’s Big Questions,” for ages 8 to 11, a girl learns that a phone assistant sometimes helps and sometimes gives a silly answer; in “Amani and the Elephant Problem,” for ages 12 to 15, students check a confident but impractical AI answer against evidence and the advice of farmers, elders, and a wildlife ranger, then pitch a beehive-fence pilot. “Once you get an answer, the first step is to confirm what you’ve gotten,” Ndegwa says. “Is it really true?”

Motivation

A Chicago special education teacher uses Gemini as a planning partner to get her students up and moving

Cheryl Graff, a special education teacher at Lake View High School in Chicago, writes on Google’s blog that she used to struggle to bridge abstract literature concepts and true hands-on learning. Now Gemini is her planning partner: before her English classes read complex stories about climate change or human rights, it helps her quickly build multi-station role-playing simulations that let students physically move through the room to solve problems and build empathy. In her Life Skills courses, it helps design smoothie recipes and dietary profiles so students can blend ingredients and practice mindful nutrition firsthand. “Gemini isn’t a replacement for active teaching,” she writes. “It’s a tool that helps me bring movement and deeper engagement into my classroom.”
