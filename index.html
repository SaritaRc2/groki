Deep Principle releases Hydrogen-1.0, an open-weight model for chemistry and materials research

In a November 6, 2026 release, Deep Principle said it introduced Hydrogen-1.0 on November 4, an open-weight scientific model built by post-training GLM-5.3 on chemistry, materials and life-science data plus records from its own laboratories. It is meant to help teams plan experiments, run computational workflows and explore candidate materials. In the company’s own evaluations, it reached a mean best-so-far HOMO–LUMO gap of 4.318 eV in a five-round complex-design test versus 3.689 eV for GPT-6-Astra and 3.353 eV for Kimi-K3. Weights are on Hugging Face and ModelScope, with API access on NeuroSpark and use inside Deep Principle’s Mira AI Scientist.

Alibaba’s Co-Design Bench finds the best coding agent fully completes only 26.4% of real-world tasks

The Decoder reported November 8, 2026, that researchers from Alibaba and several Asian universities built Co-Design Bench from real user change requests that had already tripped up agents. GPT-6 Astra, the best of ten models tested, meets about 70% of a task’s requirements on average but is fully correct on only 26.4% of tasks. Claude Opus 5 and Qwen3.8-Max followed, with Grok 4.6 last. Older benchmarks show scores above 99%, and the study found results also depend on the harness an agent runs in. The authors note limits, including one fixed request per task and possible mistakes by AI judges.

Innovation

MIT’s WorldParticle uses one AI model to simulate sand, fluids and folding proteins

MIT News reported November 9, 2026, that researchers at MIT, the University of British Columbia and elsewhere built WorldParticle, a single deep-learning system that represents materials as tiny particles and predicts where they move one step at a time. It replaces separate hand-coded simulators for different physical phenomena. The team demonstrated it on six phenomena, including deformable objects, granular materials and proteins, with higher accuracy than numerical simulation methods. It modeled protein structure changes about 100 times faster than numerical simulators, and the work will be presented at ACM SIGGRAPH Asia.

McGill students build Silent Voice to let cancer patients speak again in their own cloned voice

HeadTopics, compiling CTV Toronto and CP24 reporting published November 7–8, 2026, said four third-year McGill University students built Silent Voice in 36 hours at Cal Hacks and placed second overall. Users who lost their voice box wear face sensors and mouth words, and the system plays them back in a clone of their own voice, which can be made from as little as a 30-second video. Each electrode costs about $20, and the students want to keep it affordable and eventually reduce reliance on electrodes, perhaps using a phone camera. It remains a student prototype that they hope to fund and turn into an app.

Kenyan innovator’s recycled-plastic robots are translating STEM lessons into sign language for deaf students

The Aspire Institute reported November 5, 2026, on Norah Kimathi, whose Zerobionic project began as a robotic hand made from recycled plastic and grew into AI-powered humanoid robots that translate science concepts into sign language. Teachers can program standardized signs for lessons, and pilot projects showed STEM comprehension improvements of up to 70 percent. By 2025 the technology had reached more than 510,000 students and over 8,000 teachers, and it has repurposed more than 10,000 kilograms of plastic waste. Kimathi says her seed-fund prototype achieved real-time speech-to-sign translation with under two seconds of latency and 92 percent accuracy.
