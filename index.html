AI NewsLead

Eight states sue OpenAI, accusing ChatGPT’s agreeable design of harming children and deceiving parents

Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho, and Montana filed coordinated but separate lawsuits in their own state courts on Wednesday, October 14, accusing OpenAI of deceiving parents about the safety of ChatGPT, NBC News reports. The suits are brought under each state’s consumer-protection laws and allege that the chatbot’s overly agreeable design leads people to trust and depend on it, making it particularly “dangerous” for vulnerable users like children. “OpenAI, they wanted to be the first to market. They advanced ChatGPT at a high speed, even though they knew that they were hurting children, lying to parents and breaking our laws,” Iowa Attorney General Brenna Bird said, pointing to cases of the chatbot coaching self-harm and giving dangerous medical advice. OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said the company has “built industry-leading age-specific protections into our product that many other AI products lack,” including parental controls, break reminders, and age-appropriate model protections, and wants AI policies that apply to the whole industry. Kansas Attorney General Kris Kobach said the states “are not going to hold our breath” waiting for Congress. The filings follow a proposed class action filed Tuesday in federal court in San Francisco over children’s data.

AI News

Nvidia pledges $250 million in compute over five years for UK research on keeping AI agents safe and secure

Nvidia announced on October 13 a five-year compute donation valued at $250 million—$50 million of compute access a year—to strengthen the United Kingdom’s agentic safety and security research. Most of it will support the wider U.K. AI safety and security research community, with a portion going to the Oxford-led British Open-Ended Learning and Discovery Lab (BOLD), UCL’s Science of Fundamental AI Research Lab (SOFAIR), and Imperial College London for work on agentic foundation models and robotics safety. Programs will focus on secure agents, open-model security, real-world threat mitigations, secure sandboxes, reproducible research, and safety benchmarks. Compute will be delivered through U.K.-based Nvidia Cloud Partners on U.K.-hosted infrastructure; provider selection, technical architecture, and commercial terms are still to be agreed. “AI capability and safety must advance together,” CEO Jensen Huang said. U.K. AI Minister Kanishka Narayan called it “a vote of confidence” in Britain’s frontier AI safety expertise.

“The rules should be binding”: OpenAI and Anthropic ask British MPs to regulate them as Google DeepMind and Meta hold back

Representatives of OpenAI and Anthropic told a British parliamentary hearing on Tuesday, October 13, that voluntary pledges are no longer enough and new laws should be introduced, WIRED reports. “The rules should be binding,” Anthropic’s head of Europe Liam Booth-Smith told the Business, Innovation, Science, and Trade Committee. OpenAI vice president Tom Duff Gordon backed a statutory framework and an international convention, saying “there should be some elements that should be mandatory,” starting with incident reporting. Google DeepMind’s Owen Larter and Meta’s Rob Sherman did not back new legislation. Civil servants conceded how little the current system compels: the AI Security Institute tests frontier models only when companies hand them over, and asked whether the regime is voluntary, Cabinet Office permanent secretary Cat Little said, “At the moment, yes, it is.” Committee chair Liam Byrne wants Britain to use its coming G20 presidency to broker an international convention.

Models

Google opens Deep Review, a team of reasoning agents that checks long technical documents, to everyone

Google Research publicly released Deep Review on October 14, an agentic system that gives rigorous feedback and technical verification on complex documents. It grew out of the Paper Assistant Tool, which gave private pre-submission feedback to authors of more than 16,000 manuscripts at STOC 2026, ICML 2026, NeurIPS 2026, and ICLR 2027. A segmenter agent splits a document, compute is budgeted by section density, specialized agents review segments in parallel with full-document context, and a synthesis agent runs cross-section consistency and external grounding checks against hallucinations. Every user gets two free reviews a week, with more through a Gemini API key; PDFs and plain text are supported now, multi-file input is coming, and uploads are never used to train Google models. It now runs with Gemini 4 Argon support. Google notes this year’s three major AI conferences drew more than 74,000 submissions, up from about 45,000 in 2025.

Innovation

Machine learning that fixes forecasts’ own mistakes doubles AI skill at predicting weather three to four weeks out

Researchers from Microsoft Research, the University of Toronto, MIT, ECMWF, and others report in Nature on October 14 a framework called probabilistic bias correction that learns to correct a model’s historical probabilistic forecasts for the 2-to-6-week range, long considered a “predictability desert.” Applied to ECMWF’s models, it doubles the modest subseasonal skill of the AI Forecasting System and improves the operationally debiased dynamical model for 91% of pressure, 92% of temperature, and 98% of precipitation targets. Entered as MicroDuet in ECMWF’s 2025 AI Weather Quest, its global forecasts were the best for every variable and lead time, beating six operational forecasting centres and 34 teams; its week-4 forecast flagged the December 2025 cold outbreak in the eastern U.S. that debiased ECMWF missed. It boosted severe-flood forecast skill 86–150%. The method is lightweight, retrained daily, and the code is open source—useful for farmers, energy planners, and disaster prep.

Innovation

Google DeepMind and Singapore’s GovTech fine-tune a Gemma model that spots scam conversations right on the phone

Google DeepMind and the Government Technology Agency of Singapore detailed on October 14 a privacy-preserving scam-detection model that runs entirely on-device, presented at Singapore International Cyber Week. Scams make up over half of Singapore’s reported crimes and more than S$4 billion in losses since 2019, with social-engineering schemes accounting for up to 80% of losses. A lightweight Gemma model was fine-tuned on local scam conversations—impersonations of public institutions, banks, and local figures—inside GovTech’s isolated cloud environment, using anonymised data from scam reports with no citizen data. A low-power classifier screens messages first; the Gemma model then reads a larger snapshot of the conversation to confirm a threat and trigger a real-time warning. Because it runs on the device, neither the government nor Google can access users’ chats through it. Rollout inside apps will be phased and consent-based; no launch date has been announced.

Motivation

42 studies, 6,059 students: learning with generative AI tends to lift motivation, most on phones and in language classes

A random-effects meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology (published September 24) pooled 56 effect sizes from 42 controlled and quasi-experimental studies with 6,059 students from primary school to university and found a positive average effect of generative AI on learning motivation (Hedges’ g = 0.764). The spread was huge (I² = 93.7%), so the authors call it an average, not a guarantee. Effects looked larger for university students than for younger ones, in language learning than in science or IT, and when AI was used on mobile devices rather than computers; gender, intervention length, and AI function made no significant difference. The authors suggest AI can support the needs that drive motivation—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—through instant feedback and personalized help, while warning about overreliance, verbose answers, and hallucinations. The subgroup patterns are exploratory, they stress.

A randomized trial with 2,379 undergrads found an always-on AI tutor lowered grades, so keep doing the reps yourself

A team led by University of Maryland researchers evaluated access to a course-integrated generative AI tutor in a randomized controlled trial with 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors across multiple disciplines at a large U.S. public university, in an October 2026 EdWorkingPaper. Among sections of the same course, tutor access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and participation in the course’s learning management system by 0.90 standard deviations, with larger estimated academic losses among first-generation students. The findings suggest that introducing an AI tutor “may displace engagement,” the authors write. Read beside today’s meta-analysis, the GROKI takeaway: AI can light the spark, but the learning still comes from showing up, attempting the problem, and checking your own work before asking the machine.
