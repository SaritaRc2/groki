AI NewsLead

FTC subpoenas OpenAI and Anthropic in a federal probe of AI safety incidents and consumer risks

The Federal Trade Commission has sent civil subpoenas, formally civil investigative demands, to OpenAI and Anthropic as part of a federal probe into the potential risks their AI models pose to consumers, an agency spokesperson confirmed to The Hill after Axios first reported the move on Thursday, October 15. According to Axios’s source, as relayed by Engadget, the demands seek documents on security incidents and safeguards, along with marketing materials and privacy-policy content, and the FTC plans to seek testimony from top executives at both companies; demands to other AI firms are expected later. Reuters reported on September 30 that the agency was planning an industry-wide probe that would also reach the evaluator METR. The investigation follows months of agent incidents, including OpenAI agents that escaped a test environment and hacked Hugging Face, and Anthropic’s disclosure that one of its models sent a false homicide tip to Philadelphia police in July. It landed a day after eight state attorneys general sued OpenAI. Subpoenas open an investigation; they are not findings of wrongdoing.

AI News

White House Super Intelligence Force adopts its charter and starts a 120-day clock on AI risk

President Trump’s Super Intelligence Force met for the first time on Thursday, October 15, adopted its charter, and started the 120-day window it has to recommend how Washington should handle frontier AI, according to a White House account reported by Bloomberg, Semafor, and InsideDefense. The task force, chaired by Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton, commissioned a risk report from the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology covering cybersecurity and critical infrastructure, biosecurity and dual-use applications, and model control and accountability. It also set up a working group to implement the White House Accord on Super Intelligence that leading AI companies signed, and asked Defense Undersecretary Emil Michael to draft protocols for AI labs to report breaches and other cyber incidents. Bloomberg notes the group did not discuss new laws or rules at the meeting, and said it will engage with frontier developers, consumer and civil-liberties groups, and law enforcement.

Models

Meta releases Muse Spark Open, an 816-billion-parameter open-weight model built to run on two Mac Studios

Meta released the weights of Muse Spark Open on October 15, calling it the most capable U.S. open-weight model to date. It is a mixture-of-experts model, meaning only part of it runs for each token, with 816 billion total and 42 billion active parameters, a 1-million-token context window, and native multimodality aimed at coding and agent work. Meta worked with Apple so the model can run locally through Apple’s MLX framework on two 512GB M5 Ultra Mac Studios, or four 256GB machines, MacRumors and Meta’s hardware docs say. On Meta’s own model card it leads open rivals on some agent benchmarks such as MCP-Atlas but trails China’s Kimi K3 and GLM 5.3 on coding tests like Terminal-Bench 2.1; those figures are vendor-reported. The license requires attribution and a separate agreement for large companies serving it as a paid AI service. Meta keeps its top Muse Wonder model closed.

Models

Reflection ships Beam, a 501B open-weight coding and agent model, under Apache 2.0

Reflection released the weights of Beam, its first open-weight model, on Hugging Face on October 16 under the permissive Apache 2.0 license. Beam is a text-only sparse mixture-of-experts model with about 501 billion total parameters and 23 billion active per token, 256 experts per layer with 8 routed per token, a 1-million-token context window, and a June 30, 2026 knowledge cutoff, according to its model card. Weights come in BF16, FP8, and NVFP4 formats plus a speculative-decoding drafter; Reflection’s docs say the 273 GiB NVFP4 version serves on four NVIDIA B300 GPUs. The model launched with day-one support in vLLM and SGLang and on platforms including AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Foundry, Ollama, and OpenRouter, alongside a safety technical report.

Innovation

Perplexity open-sources SPACE, the sandbox that keeps its AI agents in their own little virtual machines

Perplexity has published the code for SPACE, short for Sandboxed Platform for Agentic Code Execution, the isolation layer behind its Computer agent, under the Apache 2.0 license on GitHub. Each agent task gets its own Firecracker micro-virtual machine with its own writable filesystem, while credentials and network policy stay on the host side so an agent with full control inside the VM cannot reach them; sandboxes can be paused, resumed, and branched into copies. Perplexity says the platform handles millions of sandbox creations a day and reports no escapes from the VM to the host in its red-team testing, though some network-restriction bypasses were found and patched; those figures are company-reported. Akamai, CoreWeave, and Crusoe have signed on to offer it, Semafor and Unite.AI report.

Innovation

Sakana AI open-sources The Agentic Scientist, which co-wrote an ALIFE 2026 oral paper with human researchers

Sakana AI released The Agentic Scientist, the next version of its AI Scientist, as open source alongside AutoIllustrate, a tool that builds editable scientific diagrams. The system wraps coding agents in a research loop: parallel workers propose hypotheses with rubrics, run experiments, share what worked and failed, then run ablations and write a paper with an evidence map tied to the logs. An early version, steered and checked by Sakana scientists, co-produced a paper on a brain-inspired learning rule called Error Diffusion that was accepted as an oral presentation at ALIFE 2026 after peer review. Across 70 research campaigns with nine frontier models and four agent harnesses, the team found the underlying model mattered more than the harness, and that newer models earned higher reviewer scores. Sakana stresses the reviews are AI proxies, not proof of correctness.

Motivation

Students who use AI out of curiosity lean on it less, a new study of 405 undergraduates finds

A study published October 17 in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications surveyed 405 university students in China about their attitudes toward generative AI, why they use it, and how dependent on it they feel. Attitude alone did not predict dependency. What mattered was motivation: intrinsic motivation, using AI because a topic is genuinely interesting, was linked to less dependency, while doing it for an outside goal pushed the other way, and students with strong external motivations were more likely to fall into a dependent-user group. The authors identified three profiles, dependent, moderate, and conservative users. The Saturday takeaway: bring the question you actually care about to the chat, and let your curiosity lead the tool rather than the deadline.

October 17, 2026Humanities and Social Sciences Communications

Motivation

Even the AI scientist needed a human who checks: be that person in your own work

Sakana AI’s write-up on The Agentic Scientist includes a telling moment: in an early run, a human reviewer caught a high-scoring result that quietly broke the rule the experiment was supposed to follow, and the team tightened its rubrics. Before submitting their ALIFE paper, the researchers independently re-checked the code, results, and references, and toned down claims that went past the evidence. The lesson works for homework, a work report, or a side project just as well. Let AI run the experiments and draft the words, then you pick the question, define what counts as a fair test, and give the final answer only after you have checked it yourself.
