Google unveils Gemini 4 Carbon, its strongest model yet, and hands it to cyber defenders first

Google DeepMind announced Gemini 4 Carbon on Wednesday, calling it its strongest model, with state-of-the-art cybersecurity and long-horizon skills. Google says it ranks No. 1 on version 5 of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index with a score of 59, and leads on SEC-bench Pro for finding vulnerabilities (88.2%), ExploitGym for exploitation (54.8%), and CWE-bench for patching (71.0%). It arrives just two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon. Rollout is deliberate: starting this week, a subset of cyber defenders gets access, then more trusted partners through Google’s Fairwind program, with developers, businesses, and consumers to follow once guardrails are tuned. Google is taking part in the U.S. government’s voluntary pre-release testing. Introductory pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, rising later to $4 and $20.

Anthropic gives every level of U.S. government a 40% discount on Claude

Anthropic and the General Services Administration expanded their OneGov deal, giving government buyers a 40% discount on token-based use of Claude Enterprise, Claude for Government, and the Claude API, with no minimum purchase. For the first time it covers state, local, tribal, and territorial governments, not just federal agencies. Anthropic estimates the discounts could save the government $1 billion and says about 500 U.S. government organizations now use Claude, with weekly Claude for Government users up about eightfold since February. The deal replaces a $1-per-user offer that expires October 31.

AI News

Cloudflare says an AI model on its platform sent 155 answers containing other customers’ data

Cloudflare disclosed that its hosted GLM-5.3 model on Workers AI returned 155 responses containing data from other customers’ requests between August 27 and September 9. A memory pool sized smaller than the model’s context length, combined with a bug in the open-source SGLang inference engine, left stale cached data from earlier requests visible when some long prompts were rejected. Cloudflare says it found no evidence of deliberate exploitation, fixed its configuration, and patched the engine; it advises anyone running SGLang to upgrade to version 0.5.22 or later.

NVIDIA’s open Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe turns meeting audio into transcripts, translations, and answers

NVIDIA introduced Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe, an open-weight speech model that handles transcription, speaker labels, and translation in one promptable model, and can answer questions about the audio, such as listing the decisions made in a meeting. It transcribes 40 languages, including seven Indic languages. NVIDIA says it ranks No. 1 on Hugging Face’s Open ASR leaderboard with about 4.3 billion active parameters, and it’s available now on Hugging Face, with quantized versions for local machines coming soon.

New startup Particle launches Particle-1, a coding model that uses about 45% fewer tokens

Particle launched with Particle-1, a software-engineering model post-trained on DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. The company says reinforcement learning in its own training environments taught the model to solve tasks with fewer tokens while keeping accuracy, using about 45% fewer tokens on average, which cuts costs and finishes tasks sooner. Particle-1 is available now through the Particle API, with other domains planned for later releases.

Innovation

AI agent swarms computer-check “the enormous theorem,” math’s longest proof

Axiom released what it calls the first complete computer-checked proof of the Classification of Finite Simple Groups, whose original proof runs about 15,000 pages across more than 500 articles by about 100 mathematicians. Over 24 days, its engineers and mathematicians directed swarms of AI agents that wrote about 43.1 million lines in the Lean proof assistant. Along the way they found 34 noteworthy deviations from the published record and a new group theory result. The code is public, but outside experts still need to rebuild it and confirm the Lean statement says what the theorem says.

A 17-year-old taught himself AI to build a pothole-spotting app, and Utah’s DOT is testing it

Ivan Turan, a senior at West High School in Salt Lake City, built Pothole AI, an iPhone app that spots potholes in real time from a dashboard-mounted phone, logging each location with a snapshot. The idea came from road damage he saw in India and a neighbor’s story of a relative killed in a pothole crash, so he taught himself machine learning to build it. He was a 2026 National STEM Festival finalist and presented to the Utah Department of Transportation, which is now testing the app. “Use the skills that they learn, not just from school but outside of school, to create these types of solutions to big, real-world problems,” he said.

Motivation

Inspired by his mom’s recovery, a 16-year-old built an AI tool to catch ovarian cancer early

Aarnav Garg, a junior at Vernon Hills High School in Illinois, was 10 when his mother was diagnosed with Stage IV ovarian cancer; she is now cancer-free. He trained OvaScan on a public collection of ultrasound images to outline tumors and judge whether they look benign or malignant, learning machine learning through hours of tutorials. His latest prototype scored 0.916 on a standard boundary-accuracy measure, in the range of published models, and it was accepted for presentation at a medical imaging conference in Edinburgh. Clinical trials would be the next step. “Her resilience really stuck with me,” he told the Daily Herald.
