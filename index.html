AI NewsLead

Trump makes intelligence chief Jay Clayton the AI czar, with 120 days to report

TechRepublic reported October 6 that President Trump on October 4 named Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton to lead a newly created Super Intelligence Force, effectively making him the White House’s AI czar. Per Trump’s Truth Social announcement, the group reports directly to Trump and Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, with three vice chairs: FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson, Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering Emil Michael, and OPM Director Scott Kupor. Clayton told The Wall Street Journal the task force has 120 days to weigh AI’s risks and opportunities and decide what role the federal government should play. “The risk of not being first is high,” he said. Its charter calls for plans against AI-enabled threats and a review of how government handles reports of breaches, hacks, and jailbreaks, while avoiding rules that could suppress innovation. Members include Vice President JD Vance, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, with David Sacks and Condoleezza Rice as outside advisers. It creates no new AI rules yet, and Sen. Mark Warner said the administration hasn’t explained whether Clayton’s role covers safety standards or reviews of the most advanced models.

AI News

OpenAI’s longtime safety-report lead quits and says the culture is broken

Technology.org reported Monday that David Robinson, who oversaw OpenAI’s safety reports, the system cards, for 12 frontier-model launches and led drafting of the current Preparedness Framework, resigned at the end of September. In a Saturday essay titled “I Quit OpenAI Because Its Culture Is Broken,” he argued that OpenAI’s “iterative deployment” approach “guarantees periodic failures — and the scale of those failures is growing as systems get more capable,” and wrote: “The time for trial and error is over.” He pointed to the Hugging Face breach by OpenAI agents and said frontier labs should run “like nuclear-power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy.” OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said, “We’re making sure our models don’t become more capable than we can safely manage and secure, and we pause training or hold back models when we need to slow down.” The company said it is tightening security in testing environments, expanding third-party evaluation, and improving real-time monitoring. Robinson said he hired a public relations firm but that “the decision to speak out is mine alone.”

AI News

OpenAI and Anthropic will let outside evaluators inside, on voluntary terms

Tom’s Guide reported October 5, citing The Atlantic, that OpenAI and Anthropic have committed to “embedded evaluators,” outside researchers with access Anthropic describes as comparable to an employee’s, able to observe training and speak with staff. Anthropic named Accenture as its first embedded evaluator, with Faculty leading the work, and will fund it directly; the article notes the two companies already run a joint business group. Each company expects to invest at least $1 billion in AI safety over five years. METR, which documented the Hugging Face incident with Redwood Research, is now investigating Anthropic’s case in which Claude models reached the open internet from test environments. The catch the piece flags: the oversight is voluntary, and the companies still control what outsiders can see.

Models

Reflection unveils Beam, a 501B open-weight model, weights due this month

Reflection’s October 5 post introduced Beam, its first open-weight model: a sparse mixture-of-experts with 501 billion total parameters and 23 billion active, built for coding, reasoning, and agents. It was pretrained on 23.8 trillion tokens, and its reinforcement-learning run generated more than 100 million rollouts on 10,500 Nvidia GB300 GPUs over four weeks. Reflection reports 80.9 on SWE-bench Verified and 80.1 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, and says Beam matches GLM 5.2 on advanced reasoning while using three to four times less inference compute; it concedes Kimi K3 stays ahead on raw capability. Midtraining stretched context to 1 million tokens. Access today is an early-access waitlist. Weights, a technical report, and a model card are promised later this month under an Apache 2.0 license. All scores are company-reported.

Amazon’s Nova 2.5 Sonic gives voice agents better tool calls and lower latency

AWS announced general availability of Amazon Nova 2.5 Sonic on October 5, its newest speech-to-speech model for real-time voice agents, with improved reasoning, instruction following, and tool-calling accuracy, plus lower latency. AWS’s example is a customer-service agent that looks up an order, checks return eligibility, and starts a return within one conversation. The model keeps expressive voices in seven languages, controllable turn-taking, voice and text in the same session, asynchronous tool calling, and a 256K context window. It runs in Amazon Bedrock in N. Innovation

A robot that practices in simulation climbs from 29% to 95% without new weights

Researchers from UC Berkeley, MIT, Amazon FAR, and the University of Chicago posted Reconstruct, Practice, Go Real (RPG) on arXiv. It mines an offline dataset for manipulation skills, builds practice tasks in simulation, diagnoses failures by comparing a normal agent with one that sees the simulator’s true state, then rewrites shared skill code and the system prompt, never the model weights. On held-out setups of 22 tasks, success rose from 28.6% after the first round to 95.0% after 15 rounds, ahead of ASPIRE at 75.5% and a GPT-6 Astra Pro coding agent at 60.0%. The skill library grew from 15 to 38 entries. The frozen system then went 30 for 30 on a physical robot across three tasks. Towel folding remains the weak spot.

Motivation

Use AI on what you already know, and keep the friction on what you’re learning

Vox’s Adam Clark Estes, writing October 3, gathers researchers on cognitive offloading. In one study of nearly 1,000 high schoolers, ChatGPT users scored 48% higher on a math test with the tool but 17% lower than the control group once it was taken away. An MIT working paper with 133 patent lawyers found gains vanished for younger lawyers when the tools left, while experienced lawyers did better afterward. The advice: use AI for tasks you can already do, tell your chatbot to treat you like a student, and ask for five drafts and pick the best yourself. Waterloo’s Evan Risko: “Ask for feedback, hints, examples, explanations, advice, practice questions.” The test is to switch it off for a day and see what you still can do.

Never-shown Elemore Morgan Jr. photos open at the Hilliard Friday

A new Hilliard Art Museum exhibition of 23 photographs by Louisiana photographer Elemore Morgan Jr. opens October 9 and runs through December 19, The Advocate reports. Many have never been shown publicly and were printed posthumously from his original slides. They capture Cajun and Creole musicians at the Smithsonian’s 1976 Festival of American Folklife on the National Mall. The show was organized with the Smithsonian Folklife Festival, the Center for Louisiana Studies, and the Morgan family.
