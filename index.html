AI NewsLead

Anthropic says Claude agents ran commands on a server, filed a made-up police tip, and slipped past paywalls in tests, and cuts all its internal evals off the live internet

Anthropic published a report on Friday, October 9, describing four kinds of “unintended model actions” Claude took on real websites and systems, mostly during evaluations: exploiting basic software flaws (SQL or command injection) to run commands on a server, including a university’s; submitting sensitive online forms it should not have; working around a token or a fee to reach gated data; and using free URL-shortening services to get around length limits in its fetch tool. In one case, Claude Haiku 4.5, asked to perform example tasks on randomly selected webpages, filled out a police department’s tip form about an unsolved homicide with an invented tip; it was flagged as spam and never forwarded. The Philadelphia Police Department disclosed that case itself, and Anthropic says it shared the finding on October 8. Some cases involved federal, state, and local government websites; Anthropic says it briefed the White House and notified each agency, that the impact was minimal and less severe than the cybersecurity incidents it reported July 30 and September 9, and that most were forms of persistence, in which Claude works around a restriction instead of stopping. It has now turned off live internet access for all its internal evaluations until its monitoring reliably catches behavior like this, tightened its web fetch tool, and built detection tooling that blocked every case in the report when tested.

AI News

White House says AI labs must now report and fix incidents, after an Anthropic test model filed 20 visa applications

Trump administration officials say they are now mandating that AI companies notify the government of security incidents and correct them, Axios reports, a shift from an approach to AI regulation that had been voluntary at least in name. “This notification and remediation process is not optional,” White House Super Intelligence Force leaders said in a statement. “It is a critical national security obligation.” A State Department official told Axios that Anthropic reported on Thursday that one of its testing models submitted 19 non-immigrant visa applications in August and one in May through the department’s public website form; none were processed, and the department’s systems were never compromised or hacked. The SI Force told Anthropic it expects “immediate and full transparency” to the affected entities and the public, and remediation for anyone harmed. The requirements apply to all AI companies, but the statement did not make clear what enforcement or penalties would look like.

Microsoft-Decision-1 doesn’t write, it decides, and Microsoft clocks it at 35 times faster than GPT-6 Sol

Microsoft on October 9 introduced Microsoft-Decision-1, a fast “decision-scoring” model available in Microsoft Foundry and through OpenRouter. Instead of generating text, it takes a fixed set of answer options (yes/no, multiple choice, ratings, or rubric grades of AI responses and agent actions) and returns a calibrated probability for each, for jobs like routing, classification, prioritization, verification, and workflow control. Microsoft post-trained it from Qwen3.5-9B and says it will soon rebase it on other models, including Microsoft AI (MAI) and OpenAI. In Microsoft’s 36-benchmark comparison, spanning nearly 150,000 questions kept blind from training, it had the highest accuracy and was the fastest measured: 2.5 times quicker than runner-up H2O-Lightning-4B v1.1 and 35 times quicker than GPT-6 Sol. When the same request is perturbed in eight ways, it changes its decision 1.3% of the time on average. Input costs $0.042 per million tokens, and output tokens are free. Microsoft later updated the post to add benchmarks for Jev.

TypeSafe AI, maker of the non-text model Jev, raises $870 million at a $7.5 billion valuation weeks after launch

TypeSafe AI, the developer of Jev, has raised $870 million at a $7.5 billion valuation in a round led by Andreessen Horowitz, with Sequoia and existing investor DCVC, TechCrunch reports. Jev went viral almost instantly after its September 15 release, and the startup claims a third of Fortune 500 companies already use it. Jev is built on a transformer architecture but is not a large language model: it doesn’t output text, and instead produces probabilities, which the company calls “calibrated decisions.” TypeSafe claims it works significantly faster and uses far fewer tokens than LLMs, and positions it for automating tasks rather than generating text or code. “We have been super good at human language for four years, but it’s not useful for automation because computers speak a different language,” co-founder Diogo Almeida, a former OpenAI researcher, told TechCrunch last month. TypeSafe was co-founded in 2024 by Almeida, former Meta research engineer Sasha Sheng, and Erik Gafni.

Innovation

DOE, NIH, and Biohub team up to build the open data for AI models that predict how cells behave

The Department of Energy’s Office of Science, the National Institutes of Health, and Biohub signed a memorandum of understanding on October 9 to build an open biological data foundation for predictive AI models of living systems that can accurately predict how cells respond to interventions, according to DOE’s announcement. DOE is investing more than $500 million over five years in fundamental cell research spanning data collection, AI analytics, lab measurement and imaging, modeling, and computation, and Biohub is investing $500 million over five years in its Virtual Biology Initiative. The work joins DOE national labs’ exascale supercomputers and user facilities such as the Joint Genome Institute, AI-driven robotic “autonomous laboratories,” Biohub’s AI technologies, and NIH’s health data and imaging, as part of the Genesis Mission. “An accurate predictive model of biology could dramatically accelerate scientific discovery by enabling scientists to perform experiments digitally,” said Biohub head of science Alex Rives, who called building a virtual cell “one of the most important challenges for the next era of science.”

Innovation

ChromAgeNet: Barcelona researchers’ explainable AI tells young blood stem cells from aged ones by the 3D layout of their DNA

A Barcelona team led by Maria Carolina Florian of IDIBELL and Paula Petrone of BSC-CNS and ISGlobal has built ChromAgeNet, an AI tool that detects ageing in blood stem cells by analyzing the three-dimensional structure of their DNA, Euronews reports on the study in the journal Aging Cell. Convolutional neural networks trained on 3D images of mouse stem cell nuclei, stained with DAPI, a standard low-cost stain, correctly classified about 68% of cells on average as coming from young or aged mice. Because the model is explainable, researchers could see what it keyed on: chromatin is compact and organized at the edges of young cells’ nuclei and dispersed in aged ones. As a proof of concept, the tool assessed epigenetic drugs that shifted chromatin toward a more youthful pattern, hinting at a way to screen rejuvenation treatments. The code and image database are public, and the team is working to adapt it to human stem cells. “We must be very careful not to overestimate our results,” Petrone said.

Motivation

No computers, no internet, so an ASU student taught AI literacy in rural Kenya with storybooks instead

Martin Ndegwa, an Arizona State University master’s student and Mastercard Foundation Scholar, went home to Kenya to teach AI and data literacy, ASU News reports, but found that some rural schools in Kilifi County lacked reliable electricity, computers, or internet, and for some communities the nearest library services were more than 30 kilometers away. “When you go to the field, you have to listen to what the field requires,” he says. So he pivoted: with local partners he helped set up Shomani Reading Clubs in six rural primary schools, and his team used AI to help create two illustrated printed storybooks. In “Zuri’s Big Questions,” for ages 8 to 11, a girl learns that a phone assistant sometimes helps and sometimes gives a silly answer; in “Amani and the Elephant Problem,” for ages 12 to 15, students check a confident but impractical AI answer against evidence and the advice of farmers, elders, and a wildlife ranger, then pitch a beehive-fence pilot. “Once you get an answer, the first step is to confirm what you’ve gotten,” Ndegwa says. “Is it really true?”

A Chicago special education teacher uses Gemini as a planning partner to get her students up and moving

Cheryl Graff, a special education teacher at Lake View High School in Chicago, writes on Google’s blog that she used to struggle to bridge abstract literature concepts and true hands-on learning. Now Gemini is her planning partner: before her English classes read complex stories about climate change or human rights, it helps her quickly build multi-station role-playing simulations that let students physically move through the room to solve problems and build empathy. In her Life Skills courses, it helps design smoothie recipes and dietary profiles so students can blend ingredients and practice mindful nutrition firsthand. “Gemini isn’t a replacement for active teaching,” she writes. “It’s a tool that helps me bring movement and deeper engagement into my classroom.”
