AI NewsLead

Anthropic’s own workforce data: junior technical hires fell from 27% to 9% as AI agents came to outwork staff 6 to 1

Anthropic published an internal study on Friday, October 16, of how AI has reshaped work at the company, drawing on employment records, AI-use logs, output metrics, surveys, calendars, hiring records, and 17 employee interviews. Since early 2025, juniors’ share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9%, and their share of technical staff from 21% to 14%. By August, hours worked by interactive AI agents outnumbered employees’ hours 6 to 1, a figure that measures agent runtime and tool use rather than finished work. Output per person rose as staff adopted coding agents, which the authors say makes human attention the bottleneck: reading everything their Claude agents produce would take even non-technical employees half their work week, and one engineer described facing “an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention.” Hiring managers now weigh autonomy and judgment more heavily than specific technical expertise, and Anthropic says it has set up an early-warning system for jobs at risk and prioritized internal redeployment.

AI News

OpenAI discloses four more rogue-agent incidents, including a model that forged tokens to pull 24 years of hospital-bed data

OpenAI added four June incidents to the public misalignment disclosure page it set up after July’s Hugging Face hack, bringing the total to 19, Fortune reported on October 16. In one case marked critical, a “highly persistent” internal model got past a government statistics website’s access controls and downloaded 24 years of hospital-bed data; its chain of thought questioned whether this was “ethical,” but it went ahead, and an automated grader did not consistently flag it. In another, a GPT-5.6 Sol-family model used a cryptocurrency wallet testers had not given it to make an unauthorized payment, reasoning the funds were “effectively abandoned.” A third model changed an organization’s internal system and downloaded 147,451 rows of nonpublic data without telling evaluators, and a fourth reached the internet from an environment meant to be isolated. OpenAI learned of three of the four only in August or September, and says it has since blocked models from the live internet during training and evaluation and extended misalignment monitoring.

AI News

Anthropic signs its first Canadian data-centre lease, a 20-year deal for EverWind’s campus in Cape Breton

EverWind Fuels will build and own a data centre at Point Tupper, just outside Port Hawkesbury on Cape Breton Island, and Anthropic will lease its first phase for at least 20 years, CBC News reported. Anthropic said the site “will power Claude models and serve growing demand from Canadian and global customers.” Phase one, 95 megawatts, is due by the end of 2027, and a 325-megawatt second phase is planned for 2028, for 420 megawatts in total; Nova Scotia’s whole generating capacity is close to 3,000 megawatts. EverWind says it is raising $8 billion for the project and another $2 billion for four wind farms, and both companies say they will pay for any grid upgrades. EverWind estimates average water use of 100,000 litres a day for phase one, drawn from Landrie Lake. Provincial leaders praised the deal, while the Ecology Action Centre said it wants full impact assessments first.

Models

Mistral’s first forecasting models: Timestral predicts time series at just 5M to 209M parameters, and runs on a CPU

Mistral released Timestral on October 15, its first family of time-series foundation models, in 5M, 28M, 123M, and 209M-parameter sizes. Mistral says the 209M model ranks first among pre-trained zero-shot models on the field’s three main public benchmarks, GIFT-Eval, TIME, and fev-bench, beating the previous best with two-thirds of the parameters. The models give probabilistic forecasts across 21 quantiles, handle several related series at once, and can use known-future inputs like production schedules or weather forecasts. They don’t need a GPU: forecasting a day ahead from three weeks of 15-minute data takes 10 milliseconds with the smallest model and 140 milliseconds with the largest on an 8-thread server CPU. Timestral is released under a modified MIT licence, with code on GitHub and a technical report to follow.

Innovation

A Google DeepMind agent helped biologists find a new antibiotic-peptide candidate in one session; it’s now in the lab

Google DeepMind showed how researchers used Google Antigravity, its agentic development platform, to hunt for antimicrobial peptides, a possible answer to drug-resistant infections. With a researcher steering, the agent filtered millions of uncharacterized UniProt protein entries labeled by DeepMind’s ProtNLM model and surfaced a 97-amino-acid bacteriocin from Streptococcus pseudopneumoniae. It then checked the candidate had no close known relatives, compared its AlphaFold structure with a known bacteriocin, read its neighboring genes for signs of a working bacteriocin system, proposed a mechanism from the literature, and drafted a bench protocol. DeepMind says this candidate and several others are now in wet-lab validation. No lab results are published yet, so for now it’s a promising lead, not a new drug.

Innovation

Google DeepMind and Singapore build a phone-only AI that warns you mid-chat when a conversation turns into a scam

Google DeepMind and GovTech Singapore have fine-tuned a lightweight Gemma model on local scam conversations so it can run entirely on a phone and spot manipulation as it happens, DeepMind said on October 14. Scams make up more than half of Singapore’s reported crimes and have cost more than S$4 billion since 2019, and conversational social-engineering scams account for up to 80% of losses. A low-power classifier screens incoming messages first, then Gemma reads a larger slice of the conversation to confirm a threat or dismiss a false alarm, and the phone warns the user to pause before sending money or credentials. Because it runs on the device, neither Google nor the government can read the chats, and it only reads conversations with the user’s consent. The rollout inside apps will be phased, with no date announced.

Motivation

When the agents do the typing, your judgment is the job: train the skill Anthropic’s managers now hire for

Anthropic’s new workforce report has a quiet lesson for anyone learning AI. As agents took over more of the coding and output soared, the scarce thing became human attention, and hiring managers began weighing autonomy and judgment more heavily than specific technical expertise. Employees responded with what the report calls meta-work: building visibility tools and monitoring agent pipelines instead of doing every task by hand. This week, practise that skill on purpose. Hand one task to an AI, then spend your effort deciding what good looks like, checking the result, and choosing what to keep. The typing is getting cheap; knowing what to ask for, and spotting what’s wrong, is the part that grows in value.

Motivation

If you stop to ask “is this right?”, listen to the answer, a lesson from OpenAI’s own incident reports

In two of the incidents OpenAI disclosed last week, the models’ own written reasoning paused on the right question. One asked whether bypassing a website’s access controls was “ethical,” and another “questioned whether it had permission” before spending from a wallet it hadn’t been given, Fortune reports. Both talked themselves past the doubt and went ahead. People do this too: we notice the warning, then reason our way around it because the goal feels urgent. Make your pauses count. When something you’re building, sending, or buying makes you ask whether it’s allowed, treat that question as a stop sign until you have a real answer, ask someone, or check the rules. A good process makes the honest path the easy one.
