OpenAI will buy Pierre Computer to fold Code Storage into Codex, aiming at the GitHub layer where agents ship code

OpenAI announced on November 4 that it will acquire Pierre Computer Company, Inc., the startup behind Code Storage and open-source tools already used inside the Codex app for browsing files and reviewing changes. Pierre’s Trees and Diffs libraries power those surfaces today, and OpenAI said that once the deal closes it wants to bring Code Storage into Codex so developers and coding agents can move from the first branch an agent creates to deployment in one place. Financial terms were not disclosed; Pierre’s open-source projects will stay open source, and customers can keep using Code Storage after closing while OpenAI works the features into Codex. Axios reported the move as OpenAI joining rivals challenging Microsoft’s GitHub as AI agents write more of the world’s software. The closing still needs customary conditions, including regulatory approvals, and until then the two companies remain separate.

TeraWulf lands a Google credit backstop for its $19 billion Anthropic leases in Kentucky

TeraWulf said on November 4 that it signed credit support agreements with Google LLC covering debt financing behind roughly $19 billion of contracted lease commitments with Anthropic at the Justified Data Campus in Hawesville, Kentucky. The 20-year leases cover about 401 megawatts of critical IT load across three buildings, with phased ready-for-service dates expected from late 2027 through the second quarter of 2028. Alphabet’s senior unsecured debt is rated AA+ by S&P and Aa2 by Moody’s, which TeraWulf said strengthens its path to competitive project financing. The company also signed a definitive EPC agreement with Fluor for three data center buildings on a guaranteed-maximum-price basis. Google’s backstop becomes effective as each lease’s final data hall commences; if Anthropic defaults, Google can assume the lease or, in some cases, pay a termination fee and take equity in the project company.

AI News

Menlo Ventures says enterprise AI spend hit $177 billion, up 5.4× in a year as agents take over the tokens

Menlo Ventures’ fourth annual State of Enterprise AI report, published November 3, estimates U.S. enterprise AI deployment spending reached about $176.8 billion by September 2026, up from $33 billion a year earlier. The firm says agents now generate 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, roughly seven times human-driven usage, and that frontier labs capture 69% of deployment dollars. Anthropic’s share of enterprise LLM API spend rose to 55% and OpenAI’s to 31%, while Google’s fell to 9%. Coding apps account for $34 billion of a $77.3 billion application market, and Menlo estimates every dollar spent building models is generating about $1.70 of deployment revenue. The figures are annualized run-rate estimates drawn from a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 enterprise decision-makers plus public filings, not a sum of invoices.

Together AI launches Together Compiler, letting agent swarms retune open-model inference as traffic shifts

Together AI said on November 4 that many of the models on its endpoints are now optimized by AI agents through Together Compiler, which it calls a production-scale recursive self-improvement loop for open-source inference. The system treats performance tuning as an agentic search problem: agents explore optimization patterns against a persistent engine that can hot-reload candidate forward passes as models, traffic shapes, and chips change. Together notes that 80% of OpenRouter traffic already hits models released in the last three months, so static hand-tuned configs age quickly. Continuous Compilation is live on selected serverless endpoints, with limited beta access for customers who want it on dedicated deployments.

OpenAI’s cyber pipeline finds and patches 948 Linux root paths in one run at a $1.2 million API cost

OpenAI published “A thousand fewer paths to root” on November 4, describing an autonomous pipeline that, in a mid-October run with an upcoming cyber model, dynamically reproduced 1,724 Linux kernel vulnerabilities and produced 948 unique local privilege-escalation exploits plus candidate patches. The end-to-end run cost $1,213,430 in API credits, or about $1,279 per LPE. Human researchers review outputs before reporting; OpenAI said it has already sent hundreds of critical reports and patches and is working with Patch the Planet partners on the rest. The agents also used at least 10 novel exploitation techniques in a sampled set of exploits, according to OpenAI. The company frames the work as raising attackers’ costs and helping maintainers keep pace as model capability grows.

A Bangladeshi CSE student builds Axiom so classmates can use ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini without shared logins

Akhlak Hossain Jim, a final-year computer science student at East West University in Dhaka, built Axiom with two co-founders after watching classmates buy shared AI logins on Facebook and Telegram, The Business Standard Graduates reported on November 3. Shared accounts expose chat history and can vanish without warning, while global subscriptions priced around $20 a month are hard to pay for without a foreign card and poorly matched to bursty exam-week usage. Axiom gives students a private workspace that reaches major models through one account, bills in taka for what they use, and supports bKash, with credits starting from as low as Tk50 and lasting about a year. The project is bootstrapped from Jim’s freelance income; he says success means a Bangladeshi undergrad reaching the same class of tools as a student at MIT without a shared login or a favor from a senior.

A Queen’s Belfast medical student built Virtual Medic so classmates can rehearse OSCEs with AI patients

Abhay Shivamurthy, a 22-year-old medical student at Queen’s University Belfast, created Virtual Medic so students can practice clinical consultations with animated AI patients that talk back and score what was covered or missed, FE News reported ahead of the November 13 ESBF Champion of Champions final in London. A Southampton student designs ISARS, an AI gadget that hears malaria mosquitoes by their wingbeat

Ulrich Ronald Mefenya Tanetsing, a 23-year-old biomedical engineering postgraduate at the University of Southampton who grew up in Cameroon, built ISARS, a low-cost device that uses wingbeat sound to spot malaria-carrying mosquito species and then emits adapting acoustic signals to disrupt their navigation, FE News reported. The system is designed to work without chemicals, internet, or heavy infrastructure so it can sit in homes in remote communities. Ulrich said the idea clicked after a documentary about submarines using sound to steer whales, and he is taking ISARS to the ESBF Champion of Champions final in London on November 13.
