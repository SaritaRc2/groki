AI NewsLead

Google gives Gemini a job: one agent that plans the work, gets its own work account, and reports back

At a Google Cloud event on Thursday, October 8, Google launched a unified Gemini agent that can be handed “objectives, not just instructions,” as Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian put it: it plans the work, uses custom skills and tools, and connects to a company’s systems, from Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, and Slack to Jira, BigQuery, Snowflake, and any Model Context Protocol server, TechCrunch reports. It picks the best model by default, but users can choose another, starting with Anthropic’s Claude. The agent gets its own Workspace account and email address, knows who is on which team, their time zones and calendars, can be tagged, emailed, or added to a group chat, and writes an audit trail in its own name; a “tasks inbox” shows its thinking, subagents, and progress. Google is starting with businesses before consumers. Sundar Pichai said Gemini has more than 1 billion monthly active users and that nearly 90% of Fortune 100 companies use Gemini Enterprise; early testers included On, Shopify, and PayPal. Google also added cost controls such as smart routing and real-time spend caps.

OpenAI reportedly tells investors its revenue run rate is near $50 billion, about $20 billion below what had circulated

The Financial Times reported October 8 that OpenAI has told investors its annualized revenue is “approaching $50 billion,” TechCrunch writes, roughly $20 billion below the nearly $70 billion figure reported a little over a week earlier. Per the FT, the higher number grew out of “attempts by OpenAI’s own investors to produce a direct comparison with Anthropic’s annualised revenues”; Anthropic counts sales made by its cloud partners in its annualized revenue, and OpenAI does not. TechCrunch notes that OpenAI raised $122 billion in a March funding round alone, that leaked 2025 financials showed about $13 billion in revenue against significantly more spending, and that its IPO, once rumored for this year, has been pushed to early 2027. TechCrunch said it had asked OpenAI for comment. The stocks desk below covers how chipmakers reacted.

USA Today and its local papers sue OpenAI, seeking more than $250 million

USA Today Co., together with local newspapers it owns, including The Tennessean, IndyStar, The Columbus Dispatch, and The Oklahoman, sued OpenAI on Thursday, claiming the company copied “hundreds of thousands” of articles to train its AI models, The Verge reports, following Reuters. The filing asks for damages of more than $250 million and says OpenAI’s unauthorized use of its content “has done real and continuing” harm to its outlets. “OpenAI’s commercial success rests on large-scale copyright infringement,” the suit claims, alleging OpenAI never asked permission. It joins copyright cases against OpenAI from The New York Times, The Intercept, Ziff Davis, CBC/Radio-Canada, Encyclopaedia Britannica, Merriam-Webster, The Seattle Times, and a coalition of nearly 400 local newspapers. OpenAI didn’t immediately respond to The Verge. These are allegations; no court has ruled on them.

StepFun’s Step 5 Preview: a 600-billion-parameter agent model, with open weights due October 15

StepFun released Step 5 Preview, its new flagship model for agentic work, on October 8, according to OpenRouter’s listing. It is a sparse mixture-of-experts model with 600 billion total parameters and 27 billion active per token, a 1-million-token context window, and vision input, and StepFun says the open weights will be released October 15. In StepFun’s own benchmarks it scores 67.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, just ahead of Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3 but behind GPT-6 Astra at 74.1% and Claude Opus 5 at 74.0%. Given 24 hours to optimize a GPU kernel, it reached 508 TFLOPS versus 493 for Claude Opus 5, StepFun says, and a Pokémon Red run has passed 3,000 turns and three Gym Badges. OpenRouter lists it at $1 per million input tokens and $2.70 per million output tokens.

Models

Odyssey-3: one world model that works a robot arm, drives a car, flies a drone, and plays GTA V

AI lab Odyssey announced Odyssey-3 on October 8, calling it its most powerful foundation world model: a system trained on a vast collection of visual observations that learns how the world behaves, so it can adapt to new tasks with relatively little extra experience. Odyssey says it has demonstrated the model in six domains: controlling robot arms, including recovery behaviors it wasn’t explicitly trained on; powering humanoid control policies developed with Flexion; driving autonomously on roads in India with a policy trained on about 20 hours of simulated driving data; generating environments where AI agents can act and learn; piloting drones indoors around obstacles; and playing Grand Theft Auto V, with early transfer to other games without extra training. “World models give physical agents a foundation of knowledge they can carry from one machine, environment, or task to another,” said CEO Oliver Cameron.

Innovation

In a real clinic, 98 patients talked with Google’s AMIE before urgent visits, and no chat had to be stopped

Researchers at Google and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center published a study in The Lancet on October 8 testing AMIE, Google’s research diagnostic chatbot, in BIDMC’s ambulatory primary care clinic. Ninety-eight patients consulted AMIE ahead of urgent care visits while supervising physicians monitored the conversations in real time, and not one had to be interrupted under predefined safety criteria. Clinicians said AMIE’s summaries helped them prepare for visits in 75% of cases and influenced their approach to care in more than half, and its differential diagnoses matched the doctors’ final diagnoses 90% of the time. Google calls it its first publication in The Lancet’s main journal, and says larger clinical trials are needed to assess patient-facing AI at scale.

Innovation

AWS open-sources a Physical AI Toolchain to take robots from data to deployment

Amazon Web Services launched an open-source Physical AI Toolchain on October 8 that packages AWS services and NVIDIA’s physical AI software into a single workflow, The Robot Report reports. It covers five steps, synthetic data generation, model training, simulation and validation, edge deployment, and continuous improvement, using Amazon SageMaker and AWS IoT Greengrass alongside NVIDIA’s Isaac Sim, Isaac Lab, Isaac GR00T, and Cosmos; companies can use the pieces individually or end to end, and data from robots in the field flows back to the cloud to keep improving models. “We’re trying to make this easy button,” said AWS’s Sri Elaprolu, who said it draws on lessons from Amazon’s more than 1 million robots and stays hardware-neutral. He pointed to Japanese company Telexistence, which has deployed more than 300 humanoids in convenience stores.

Motivation

Teachers in India got two hours a week back from an AI lesson planner, and spent it on hands-on learning

Researchers at Cornell and Microsoft Research India evaluated Shiksha Copilot, an AI system that turns textbook material into grade-appropriate lesson plans in English and Kannada, with more than 1,000 fifth- to 10th-grade teachers in Karnataka, the Cornell Chronicle reported October 8. Curators check every plan, teachers customize them, and guardrails keep examples local, like dosas instead of pizza. Teachers said it saved them an average of two hours a week and let them swap lecturing for hands-on activities such as collecting local leaves with different vein patterns. “Usually, whenever someone comes with something new, it just adds to our workload, but this is a burden-releasing concept,” said a science teacher with 20 years of experience. About 30,000 teachers in Telangana are using it this year, and the researchers found tailoring a tool to local needs can be up to 80% of the work. Today’s move: hand one chore to AI, then spend the time you win on the hands-on part.

Today at the Hilliard: the Revolution and Evolution symposium, with the Smithsonian

Festivals Acadiens et Créoles’ pre-festival symposium is today, Friday, October 9, starting at 9 a.m. on the second floor of the Hilliard Art Museum, 710 East St. Mary Boulevard (the festival’s page lists a 4 p.m. finish in its heading and 5 p.m. in its program notes). The theme is “Revolution and Evolution: Celebrating 250 Years of Cultural Fusion on the Bayou,” from the Center for Louisiana Studies, the festival, the Smithsonian, and the Hilliard. Panels cover the Indigenous, African, and European currents that shaped South Louisiana, how radio and social media spread Cajun and Creole culture, Ralph Rinzler’s 1960s Louisiana recordings, how the music was recorded and shared with the world, and Louisiana’s role in the American Revolution, with speakers including Christine Balfa, Lt. Barry Ancelet shows rare 1976 Smithsonian festival footage at lunch, and the day closes with a reception for the Elemore Morgan Jr. exhibit.
